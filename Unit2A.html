    In the matter of student expression

    Where do your rights end — and the schoolhouse gate begin?

    An interactive walk through speech, press, religion, and assembly rights for students, one Supreme Court case at a time.

    Case 1 of 5

    1943

    West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette

    Background Information

    In 1942, the West Virginia Board of Education required public schools to include salutes to the flag by teachers and students as a mandatory part of school activities. The children in a family of Jehovah's Witnesses refused to perform the salute and were sent home from school for non-compliance. They were also threatened with reform schools used for criminally active children, and their parents faced prosecution for causing juvenile delinquency.

    The Question Before the Court

    Did the compulsory flag salute for public schoolchildren violate the First Amendment?

    The Ruling

    In a 6–3 decision, the Court overruled its decision in Minersville School District v. Gobitis and held that compelling public schoolchildren to salute the flag was unconstitutional.

    Case 1 — Impact

    What Does This Mean For Students?

    The Takeaway

    Students cannot be compelled, or forced, to stand for the Pledge of Allegiance. National symbols, like the flag, are not more important than a student's First Amendment rights.

    Case 2 of 5

    1986

    Bethel School District No. 403 v. Fraser

    Background Information

    In 1983, high school student Matthew Fraser gave a speech nominating a classmate for student elective office at a voluntary school assembly. His speech was filled with elaborate, graphic sexual metaphors. Some students in the audience hooted, yelled, and mimicked the acts he described, while others looked confused or embarrassed. School administrators suspended Fraser for several days and removed his name from consideration as a graduation speaker, citing the school's rule against obscene, profane, or disruptive conduct. Fraser sued, arguing that punishing him for his speech violated his First Amendment rights.

    The Question Before the Court

    Does the First Amendment prevent a school from disciplining a student for giving a lewd speech at a school assembly?

    Case 2 — The Ruling

    What Did the Court Decide?

    Before we reveal the ruling

    How do you think the Court ruled?

    Type at least 5 words to continue.

    The Ruling

    In a 7–2 decision, the Court held that the First Amendment did not prevent the school from disciplining Fraser for his speech. Schools may prohibit vulgar and lewd language because teaching students the boundaries of socially appropriate behavior is part of a public school's educational mission — even when that speech causes no substantial disruption.

    Case 2 — Impact

    What Does This Mean For Students?

    The Takeaway

    While students do enjoy a level of free speech, that does not include inappropriate language, and schools are within their rights to punish students for this language.

    Case 3 of 5

    2007

    Morse v. Frederick

    Background Information

    In 2002, the Olympic Torch Relay passed through Juneau, Alaska, and Juneau-Douglas High School released students from class, under teacher supervision, to watch it go by. Standing across the street on public property, student Joseph Frederick unfurled a large banner reading "BONG HiTS 4 JESUS" as the cameras passed. Principal Deborah Morse crossed the street, confiscated the banner, and suspended Frederick, believing the banner promoted illegal drug use. Frederick sued, arguing that punishing him for the banner violated his First Amendment rights.

    The Question Before the Court

    Does the First Amendment allow public schools to restrict student speech that appears to promote illegal drug use at a school-supervised event?

    Case 3 — The Ruling

    What Did the Court Decide?

    Before we reveal the ruling

    How do you think the Court ruled?

    Type at least 5 words to continue.

    The Ruling

    In a 5–4 decision, the Court held that schools may restrict student speech at school-supervised events when that speech is reasonably viewed as promoting illegal drug use. Because deterring drug use among students is an important interest of schools, Frederick's suspension did not violate the First Amendment.

    Case 3 — Impact

    What Does This Mean For Students?

    The Takeaway

    Schools can legally stop students from wearing clothes, holding signs, or sharing messages that promote the use of any substance that is illegal or prohibited for minors. That is why you can't wear a dispensary shirt, a beer hat, a vodka hoodie, etc. (Yes, all of these are examples from BVA.)

    Case 4 of 5

    1990

    Westside Community Schools v. Mergens

    Background Information

    Bridget Mergens, a student at Westside High School in Omaha, Nebraska, asked the school for permission to form a Christian club that would meet after school, just like the school's other extracurricular clubs, to read and discuss the Bible, pray, and share fellowship. The school denied her request, concerned that allowing a religious club to meet on campus would violate the Establishment Clause. Under the federal Equal Access Act, any public school that receives federal funding and allows even one "noncurriculum-related" student club to meet must give all other student groups the same access, regardless of the religious, political, or philosophical content of their speech. Mergens sued the school district.

    The Question Before the Court

    Does the Equal Access Act violate the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment by requiring public schools to allow student religious clubs to meet on campus on the same terms as other clubs?

    Case 4 — The Ruling

    What Did the Court Decide?

    Before we reveal the ruling

    How do you think the Court ruled?

    Type at least 5 words to continue.

    The Ruling

    In an 8–1 decision, the Court held that the Equal Access Act did not violate the Establishment Clause. Because the club would be student-initiated and student-led, not sponsored or directed by the school, and would meet during non-instructional time, allowing it to meet did not amount to the school endorsing religion.

    Case 4 — Impact

    What Does This Mean For Students?

    The Takeaway

    Schools cannot discriminate against student groups based on religious, political, or philosophical content of their speech. This does not violate the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment as long as it is student-led and voluntary, during non-instructional time, and does not have active school or faculty sponsorship/direction.

    Case 5 of 5

    1969

    Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District

    Background Information

    In December 1965, a group of students in Des Moines, Iowa, decided to wear black armbands to school to protest the Vietnam War and support a truce. When school officials learned of the plan, they adopted a policy banning armbands and warned that any student who refused to remove one would be suspended. Mary Beth Tinker, John Tinker, and Christopher Eckhardt wore the armbands anyway and were suspended until they agreed to return without them. Their parents sued on their behalf, arguing that the school had violated the students' First Amendment rights.

    The Question Before the Court

    Does a school's prohibition against students wearing armbands as a form of symbolic protest violate the free speech protections of the First Amendment?

    Case 5 — The Ruling

    What Did the Court Decide?

    Before we reveal the ruling

    How do you think the Court ruled?

    Type at least 5 words to continue.

    The Ruling

    In a 7–2 decision, the Court held that the armbands were a form of pure speech, separate from any conduct of the students wearing them, and that they caused no disruption to school activities. The Court famously declared that students do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate," and ruled that schools may only restrict student speech when it would substantially disrupt the educational process or interfere with the rights of others.

    Case 5 — Impact

    What Does This Mean For Students?

    The Takeaway

    Students can express personal opinions, as long as it is not disruptive. Schools can step in if/when a student's expression of personal opinions becomes disruptive.
